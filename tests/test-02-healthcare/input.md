## 2. Research Interview Record

**Consultant B:** Director Wu, could you give an overview of the outpatient situation at the center?

**Director Wu:** We are a 1,200-bed regional center handling roughly 6,500 outpatient visits per day across 42 clinical departments. Our biggest strategic concern is patient experience. The average total time a patient spends in the hospital for a single outpatient visit is 172 minutes, of which only about 8 minutes is actual consultation time with the doctor. The rest is queuing — for registration, payment, tests, and medication collection, each at separate windows on different floors.

**Consultant B:** Head Nurse Sun, what does this look like from the front line?

**Head Nurse Sun:** Every morning we have queues forming at 6:30 AM before registration opens at 8:00. Elderly patients struggle the most — about 35% of our patients are over 60 and many cannot use the existing phone app, which anyway only covers registration, not payment or reports. Patients ask nurses for directions constantly; our triage desks handle around 900 wayfinding and process questions per day, which is not clinical work at all. We also see repeated tests because paper reports get lost between departments — patients sometimes repeat a blood test simply because the previous result cannot be located.

**Consultant B:** Manager Guo, what is the systems situation?

**Manager Guo:** Our HIS, LIS, PACS, and EMR systems come from 4 different vendors and were implemented between 2015 and 2022. Integration is partial. Lab results reach the EMR with an average delay of 40 minutes because of an outdated interface engine. Doctors complain that they spend, by our internal survey, an average of 3.1 hours per day on documentation — outpatient notes, admission records, discharge summaries — much of it repetitive structured text. Our EMR completion audits show 22% of records have missing or delayed entries, which creates both clinical risk and insurance reimbursement rejections. Last quarter, documentation-related reimbursement rejections cost us approximately 2.4 million in denied claims.

**Consultant B:** What about clinical staff attitudes toward new technology?

**Director Wu:** Doctors are open to anything that genuinely reduces documentation burden, but they are deeply skeptical after a failed voice-input pilot three years ago that added work instead of reducing it. Any new solution must demonstrably save time in the first month, or adoption will fail. Also, patient data security is non-negotiable — everything must remain inside the hospital private network and comply with national health data regulations, and we will require a full security review before any deployment.

**Consultant B:** What outcomes would leadership consider success?

**Director Wu:** Four targets. Cut average patient in-hospital time from 172 minutes to under 90. Reduce doctor daily documentation time from 3.1 hours to under 1.5 hours. Bring EMR record completeness above 95%. And cut documentation-related reimbursement rejections by at least 70%. We report to the municipal health commission annually and these indicators are all part of the public hospital performance assessment.

## 3. Customer Key Pain Points

1. Patient in-hospital time averages 172 minutes per visit with only ~8 minutes of consultation — queuing at separate windows dominates the experience.
2. Elderly patients (35% of volume, over age 60) cannot effectively use the existing limited-function app; digital services exclude the largest patient group.
3. Triage nurses handle ~900 non-clinical wayfinding/process questions daily, displacing clinical work.
4. Four-vendor system landscape (HIS/LIS/PACS/EMR) with partial integration; lab results delayed an average of 40 minutes to the EMR; lost paper reports cause repeated tests.
5. Doctors spend 3.1 hours per day on largely repetitive documentation; a previous voice-input pilot failed and created adoption skepticism.
6. 22% of EMR records have missing or delayed entries, creating clinical risk and approximately 2.4 million per quarter in documentation-related reimbursement rejections.
7. Strict security constraints: all data must remain on the hospital private network and comply with national health data regulations, with a mandatory security review before deployment.
